Source: product-growth newsletter, Feb 2026 issue

The argument: onboarding checklists work not because they teach the product but
because they convert an open-ended space into a finite list. The user stops
asking "what can this do" and starts asking "what's left".

Supporting points from the piece:
- Completion rates jump most on the *second* item, not the first — the first is
  usually free (signing up), so item two is where intent is really revealed
- Checklists with more than seven items perform worse than ones with four
- Showing progress as "2 of 4" beats a progress bar, because the denominator is
  legible

Counterpoint they raise and then dismiss a bit too quickly: checklists optimise
for the metric they display. If the checklist says "connect a folder", people
connect a folder and leave.

Relevant to the onboarding work. The "finite list" framing is the useful bit.
